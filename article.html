We sell the parts.
We model them too.

A supplier usually hands you a photo and a part number. We would rather hand you the geometry — measured, materialled and exported, so you can check it before it reaches site.

01 / Why we model

The catalogue
is evidence.

Rapid Volt supplies cable, containment, fixings and lighting to contractors across the UAE. The range is broad, but a spec sheet can only tell you so much. A 3 mm joint plate looks simple until you need to know where the slots sit; a gland looks familiar until the earth tag has to clear a enclosure wall.

So we rebuilt five products as real geometry. Not a box with a texture, not a generic stand-in — the actual part, at real millimetre dimensions, with separate materials for steel, brass, rubber and lens. Open one in the viewer, orbit it, switch to wireframe, and download the file if it helps your drawing.

There is no account and no paywall. The models are part of the service.

02 / How it is made Measure → model → export
01

Measure the real part

Every dimension starts in millimetres: the 300 × 80 mm joint plate, the 13.4 mm cable OD, the 20 mm saddle. Where a supplied CAD file existed — the 15 W downlight — we used it as the base and cleaned the mesh.

02

Build it as geometry

Threads, slots, ribs, knurls and the cutaway layers of the cable are modelled, not painted. The cable is a true section: conductor, XLPE, bedding, steel wire armour and oversheath, each with its own surface.

03

Material it honestly

Hot-dip galvanised steel, zinc-passivated pressed steel, brass and rubber get different roughness and metallic values. The downlight lens is a separate transmissive disc, so the render reads as a fitting rather than a grey lump.

04

Export it clean

One source file produces all three downloads. GLB for the web viewer, FBX for DCC and CAD workflows, STL for printing and quick checks. Files are metres in scene units, so they drop into a drawing at the right size.
